ISU Graduate College

As a Graduate Administrative Assistant, I designed and developed pages for the Iowa State University Graduate College website. Before changing anything, I set out to learn what graduate students were actually trying to find, and why so many of them still ended up emailing the office instead.

Role
Graduate Administrative Assistant
Focus
User research, web design & development
Users
Current & prospective grad students
Timeline
2023 – 2024
Context

A website organized around the office, not around the student.

The Graduate College site is structured by audience (Future Students, Current Students, Faculty) and, inside each, by the office's own categories: programs, funding, forms. That works when you already know where something lives. Many graduate students don't, especially international students, who are new to both the university and the U.S. system.

What are graduate students really looking for on the Graduate College website, and what stops them from finding it on their own?

Who uses it

About one in three ISU graduate students is an international student.

Before talking to anyone, I looked at who the site serves. International students make up a large share of ISU's graduate population, and their questions come with rules attached: visa status, work-hour limits, and paperwork that domestic students never see.

How I researched it

I listened where the questions were already happening.

Working inside the Graduate College gave me direct access to the questions students were asking. I combined that with a structured look at the site itself and outside data, so findings didn't depend on a single source.

01
Student conversations
Short, open-ended conversations with graduate students: "What were you looking for? Where did you look first? What did you do when you couldn't find it?"
02
Question log
Tracked and tagged the questions students brought to the office by email and in person, to see which topics came up most.
03
Content & navigation audit
Walked the site's menus and key pages as a student would, including a keyboard-only pass, noting dead ends, duplicates, and handoffs to other offices.
04
Desk research
Compared what I heard with ISU enrollment data and international student data, to check that the patterns weren't just a few loud voices.

What students look for

The top question wasn't "which program?" It was "how do I pay for it?"

The most common thread was funding: scholarships, fellowships, and research assistantships. Students wanted to know what was available, whether an assistantship covers tuition, and who to contact. For international students the stakes are higher: F-1 status generally limits them to on-campus work, capped at 20 hours a week during the semester, and a half-time assistantship already uses all 20 of those hours. Funding is both how they pay for school and how they work.

These are research-heavy programs, where doctoral students typically depend on research and teaching assistantships. So it made sense that funding came up first. A student asking "Is there funding?" is often really asking, "Can I afford to stay, and am I allowed to work for it?"

Key insights

Students weren't failing to search. The answers were split across offices.

  1. 01
    One question, four places to look
    To answer "How do I fund my degree?", a student needed the Graduate College (scholarships and fellowships), department pages (research assistantships), the Graduate Catalog (tuition rules), and, for international students, ISSO (work-hour limits). No single page connected them, so students asked staff to do it for them.
    Evidence: question log, page audit
  2. 02
    Audience menus don't fit international students
    "Future Students" and "Current Students" assume needs change on enrollment day. International students need visa and funding information across both stages, so they bounced between menus.
    Evidence: student conversations, navigation audit
  3. 03
    "Is it in Workday or is it a PDF?"
    After the move to Workday, the forms page mixed Workday tasks with downloadable PDFs. Students knew the form's purpose but not which system to open, and the "(Workday)" tag was easy to miss.
    Evidence: question log, content audit
  4. 04
    Menus that don't follow the keyboard
    In a keyboard-only pass, focus moved to "Current Students" while the "Future Students" menu stayed open, so what was highlighted didn't match what was showing.
    Evidence: accessibility walkthrough

From research to design

Organize around the student's task, not the office's structure.

Each insight shaped a part of the site I then designed and built, working with my supervisor on priorities and review.

From insight 01
One funding hub
A single page for scholarships, fellowships, and assistantships: what's available, what it covers, how to apply, and the work rules for international students, in the order a student needs them.
From insights 02 & 03
"I want to…" entry points
Task-based shortcuts ("find funding", "submit a form") that work for future and current students alike, with each form clearly labeled Workday or PDF.
From insight 04
Accessible menus
Menus that close when focus leaves and open with Enter or Space, so keyboard and screen-reader users see what they're on.
Outcome

What I shipped.

I designed and developed updates across the four areas the research pointed to, with my supervisor guiding priorities and reviewing each change before it went live.

  • Funding pagesScholarships, fellowships, and assistantships organized around the questions students actually asked: what's available, what it covers, and how to apply.
  • Student FormsA cleaner forms page where each item clearly shows whether it's done in Workday or as a PDF.
  • International student informationClearer paths for international students, connecting Graduate College content with the ISSO rules they need.
  • Navigation & menusMenu structure and labels revised, with attention to keyboard and screen-reader behavior.

The biggest lesson: students rarely say "I can't find the page." They say "Is there funding for me?" Listening to the real question, then tracing where its answer lived, showed that the problem wasn't the search box. It was how the information was split up.
