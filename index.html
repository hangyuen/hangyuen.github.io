Augmented Reality

Howl's Moving Castle

The walking castle from the Studio Ghibli film, standing in your own room.

Step around it.

This augmented reality experience brings the walking castle from Studio Ghibli's Howl's Moving Castle (Hayao Miyazaki, 2004) into the viewer's own space. Once the app recognizes an image target, the castle appears in the room and can be explored from any angle: clouds drift past, smoke curls from its chimneys, and fire flickers beneath it as it strides forward.

From 2D film
to real-time 3D.

I translated the film's 2D design into a fully modeled 3D asset in Autodesk Maya, textured it with Substance Designer and Substance Painter, and built the experience in Unity. Because people could walk around the castle and lean in close, every surface and mechanical detail had to hold up from any viewing angle while still running smoothly in real time.

Tested in the wild

Many visitors described it as unexpectedly moving.

Exhibited at Lockwood Cafe in Ames, the piece doubled as an in-the-wild test. Watching visitors and listening to their feedback, a clear pattern emerged: people moved freely around the castle, assigned their own meaning to the space, and built a clearer sense of spatial relationships simply through their own exploration. Many described it as unexpectedly moving. The takeaway is that AR's intuitive visual cues have real potential for spatial learning, and for reshaping how people move through and interact with the spaces around them.

A Thousand Li

Step inside a Northern Song landscape painting.

Vast landscapes,
tiny people.

Northern Song dynasty (960–1127) "Shan Shui" painters like Fan Kuan and Guo Xi placed tiny human figures within vast landscapes to express humility and a respectful relationship with nature. Today, most people meet these works as flat images in a textbook or behind museum glass, where that sense of scale is hard to actually feel.

Step inside
the painting.

A Thousand Li is a VR learning experience designed to close that gap. Developed as my MFA & MS in Human-Computer Interaction thesis, it reconstructs a historic landscape painting as a space people can step inside, and explores how immersive interaction can make art history and environmental education more engaging and accessible.

Play first,
learn on demand.

The core design principle was play first, learn on demand. Users pilot a boat across the painting, watch petals drift on the wind, follow fish weaving through the water, and climb a path toward a mountain temple. I designed and built each mechanic, from particle systems driving the petals to scripted fish behavior, so the painting invites action instead of passive viewing. Optional info points let curious users dig into the art history of the era without interrupting those who just want to explore, and reframe an 11th-century way of seeing nature through a contemporary lens on our place in the environment.

Papel

A 3D animated short about a lonely axolotl child and the family they draw.

A story told
in drawings.

In 2021, a team of artists from diverse backgrounds came together to create Papel (2022), a 3D animated short film directed by Nancey Fang. The story follows a lonely axolotl child who draws pictures of their absent parents and dreams of happier days. Through their art, they find that a loving family may not be as far away as they think.

Producer, and hands-on.

The producer role oversaw the production pipeline end-to-end and coordinated cross-departmental workflows, with hands-on contributions across animation, visual effects, environment modeling, surfacing and texturing, compositing, and sound design. This combination of production leadership and creative execution shaped the film's visual and narrative cohesion, from early planning through final delivery.

ISU Graduate College

To learn what their funding covered, students had to check four places. I redesigned the site so the answer starts in one.

About a 10-minute read, so settle in.
01The problem

The site followed the office's structure. Students showed up with questions.

Pages were grouped by audience (future students, current students, faculty) and then by the office's own categories. That works for staff who already know where things are. New graduate students usually don't, so many of them just emailed us.

Research question
What are graduate students actually looking for, and what keeps them from finding it on their own?

02Research

How I did it. 25 short conversations and 73 emails.

I talked with most students at orientation, as part of regular conversation, and the rest during quick chats at the front desk. Of the 25, 17 were international, 16 were Ph.D. students and 9 were Master's students.

With only a few minutes each, I kept to one main question: "Where did you look first?" The answer told me which page students expected to have the answer, and whether it actually did.

03What I found

Funding was the biggest gap. It wasn't the only one.

Going in, I expected international students to ask mostly about visa paperwork. Instead, a lot of their questions were about money. Some wanted to take fewer classes so they could work more. Others felt they were already working more than 20 hours a week and worried about their visa. That isn't always a problem, since it depends on their program and level, but no single page explained it. That changed where I focused. I stopped treating funding and work rules as separate topics.

Each finding below looks at the problem from a different distance: a student's whole time at ISU, a single question, one student's first semester, and the site's menus.

      6.2Limitations

      Limitations. What this research can and can't tell you.

      Sample
      Small, and more international than ISU
      17 of my 25 students were international, about twice the campus share. That fit the project's focus, but domestic students may rank these needs differently.
      Depth
      Short, informal conversations
      Five-minute chats were good for spotting common questions, but not for digging into why.
      Evidence
      No formal before/after metrics
      I can show what shipped, but I can't yet show that it reduced emails.
      Desk research
      Benchmark data added in 2026
      Peer scores and cost figures come from public sources I reviewed while writing this case study.
      If I did it again
      Next time, I'd start counting earlier and test before launch.
      Research ops
      Tag the inbox from day one
      Tag every student email as it comes in, so I'd have real counts instead of relying on what I remembered.
      AI collaboration
      Let AI do the first sort
      An AI model could group anonymized emails by topic as they come in. I'd still review every group by hand before using it.
      Testing
      Test with students before launch
      I'd sit down with 5 students and run the tasks in 6.1 on a draft of the funding pages, so I could fix problems before the pages went live.
      Recruiting
      Reach students who never write in
      Orientation reached a lot of new students. Next time I'd add longer follow-up sessions through departments, plus a 2‑minute survey.
